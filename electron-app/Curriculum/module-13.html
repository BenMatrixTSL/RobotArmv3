Safety Standards & Design for Safety

How international standards shape robot design, and how to apply systematic safety thinking to the arm you are using.

Theory

Key Safety Standards for Robots

Industrial robots are governed by a hierarchy of international standards. These define how robots must be designed, installed, and operated to protect workers:

StandardScope
ISO 10218-1Robot design and manufacture — what the robot builder must provide
ISO 10218-2Robot installation and work cell — what the integrator must ensure
ISO/TS 15066Collaborative robots (cobots) — force and speed limits for human contact
EN 60204-1Electrical safety of machines — wiring, guarding, emergency stop design
PUWER 1998UK law — Provision and Use of Work Equipment Regulations
Note: This arm is an educational device, not CE-marked industrial equipment. However, the same safety principles apply at any scale.
Theory

The Hierarchy of Controls

When designing for safety, engineers work through a priority hierarchy — eliminating hazards is always preferred over warning about them:

1 Eliminate — Remove the hazard entirely (e.g. redesign so the motion is not needed)
2 Substitute — Replace with a less dangerous alternative (e.g. slower motor)
3 Engineering controls — Guards, interlocks, software limits, force limiting
4 Administrative controls — Procedures, training, supervision, permit-to-work
5 PPE — Personal protective equipment (last resort — relies on human compliance)

In this arm, the Dead Zones tab provides an engineering control (level 3). Your lab rules about not reaching into the operating envelope are administrative controls (level 4).

🚫
Dead Zones Tab — Safety Parameters
Each dead zone is a named rectangular XYZ exclusion volume with an enable/disable toggle. In the safety hierarchy, dead zones are a Level 3 Engineering Control — they are enforced automatically by the server and do not rely on the operator remembering to avoid a specific area. Enable the Dead Zones overlay in 3D Visualisation to verify that defined zones correspond to physical obstacles in the lab layout. A zone defined incorrectly (wrong coordinates, wrong size, or accidentally disabled) provides no protection — verify the visual overlay against the physical space.
🔐
Control Session — Level 3 Engineering Control
The software control session mechanism is an engineering control (Level 3 in the hierarchy). Only one WebSocket client at a time can issue movement commands. Key properties:
  • A 5-minute idle timeout auto-releases control if no movement is commanded — preventing an abandoned session from blocking others
  • A disconnecting client immediately releases control
  • Control can only be forcibly taken from another client using an explicit Force Take Control — and the displaced client is notified
In a multi-user lab environment, this prevents two students from simultaneously commanding conflicting movements. Classify it alongside dead zones in your hierarchy of controls worksheet.
Theory

Speed & Force Limiting

One of the most important safety parameters in any robot is how fast it moves and how much force it can exert. These two quantities determine the severity of a collision:

Energy = ½ × m × v²    Force = m × a

Doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy at impact. This is why cobots under ISO/TS 15066 have strict speed limits when humans are nearby.

🔌 Speed limiting

Reducing maximum joint speed (via the Settings tab or G-code feed rate F parameter) reduces both cycle time and collision energy.

⚡ Torque/current limiting

The ST3215 servos have a hardware current limit built into the servo IC — this caps the force the joint can exert. There is no current-limit slider in the Settings tab; the closest software-accessible safety control for overload is the speed limit.

⚠️ Even at low speeds, a robot arm can cause injury if a finger or hand is trapped between a moving link and a fixed object. Treat the arm with the same respect as any powered machinery.
🌡️
Thermal Protection — Layered Engineering Controls
The system has thermal protection at two levels — a useful example of layered engineering controls:
  • Level 1 (hardware): the ST3215 servo IC has a built-in thermal fuse / shutdown — the servo cuts power if it overheats, regardless of software state
  • Level 3 (software): the server detects an overtemperature fault from the servo's telemetry and enforces a 30-second cool-down period for that joint, refusing further move commands until it has cooled. The LED strip switches to flashing orange to signal the fault
Ask: which control is preferred in the hierarchy, and why is the hardware-level control retained even when the software-level control exists?
Worksheet

Activity — Safety Audit of the Arm

Carry out a systematic safety review of the arm setup in your lab, applying the hierarchy of controls.

  • Identify three specific hazards associated with this arm (e.g. struck by moving link, trapped fingers, tripping on cables). Write each below.
  • For each hazard, classify it using the hierarchy: which existing controls are in place? Which level of the hierarchy are they at?
  • Open the Dead Zones tab. Check that joint limits are set appropriately. Does the current limit prevent the arm reaching any fixtures or people in your lab layout?
  • Open the Settings tab. Find the Linear Speed and Movement Mode parameters and record their current values. Note: there is no current-limit slider here — the ST3215 servo's current limit is a hardware parameter. The closest software-accessible safety control for overload is the speed limit.
  • Open the Joint Control tab. Command a fast move (high speed) and observe the behaviour. Then set speed to 20% and repeat. Describe the difference in collision risk.
  • Write a short (half-page) safe operating procedure (SOP) for using this arm in your lab. Include: who may operate it, pre-use checks, what to do if something goes wrong, and how to safely power it down.

SOP Template — Connection and Control Workflow
Your SOP should include this specific sequence for using the application safely:
  1. Open the application, navigate to Connection tab, enter Pi IP address (or use Auto-Detect), connect
  2. Observe the LED strip — confirm it shows solid green (connected and ready) before proceeding
  3. Press Take Control — confirm the status bar shows you have control
  4. Enable torque only when ready to move and everyone is clear of the working envelope
  5. After completing work: press E-Stop or run the Home sequence, then Disable Torque, then press Release Control
  6. Do not leave the session with control held — another user will be locked out

Settings Tab — Confirmed Parameters
Relevant parameters in the Settings tab for safety analysis:
  • Movement Mode: PTP (point-to-point, faster) or Linear Cartesian (straight-line end-effector path, slower)
  • Linear Speed (mm/s): maximum speed for Linear Cartesian moves
  • Linear Step Size (mm): waypoint spacing for linear interpolation (smaller = smoother path, more computation)
  • Status Watchdog Interval: fallback poll period if the server stops pushing updates
There is no explicit current-limit slider — the ST3215 servo's current limit is a hardware parameter. The closest software-accessible safety control for overload is the speed limit: lower speed → lower peak current draw → lower risk of thermal overload.
So What?

Safety Is an Engineering Responsibility

In UK law (Health and Safety at Work Act 1974, PUWER 1998), employers have a duty to ensure that work equipment is safe. But the engineer who designs the system shares moral — and sometimes legal — responsibility for how safe it is to use.

The hierarchy of controls, risk assessments, and safety standards exist because history has shown that relying on people to "be careful" is not enough. Engineering out the hazard is always better than managing it with rules and warning signs.

Think about: If you were asked to deploy this arm in a public exhibition where members of the public (including children) could get close to it, what additional controls would you need? At which levels of the hierarchy?
