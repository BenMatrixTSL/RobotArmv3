Theory

Why Maintain a Robot?

An industrial robot may run two or three shifts a day for ten years or more. Over that time cables flex millions of times, bearings and gears wear, fixings loosen, and dust and heat build up. Maintenance keeps the robot safe, accurate and available; calibration makes sure it still goes exactly where it is told.

In the UK this is also a legal duty. The Provision and Use of Work Equipment Regulations 1998 (PUWER), which you met in Module 14, require work equipment to be maintained in an efficient state, in efficient working order and in good repair, and any maintenance log to be kept up to date.

Three approaches to maintenance

🔧 Corrective (reactive)

Fix it when it breaks. Cheap to plan, but breakdowns are unplanned, often at the worst moment, and can damage other parts.

📅 Preventive (planned)

Inspect and service on a fixed schedule - daily, weekly, every 2,000 hours. Catches most problems early, but some parts are replaced before they need to be.

📈 Predictive (condition-based)

Use data to service only when a part shows signs of wear - for example a servo whose temperature or load slowly rises week by week. Needs monitoring (Module 11).

Measuring whether maintenance works: engineers track downtime (hours the robot could not run), MTBF (mean time between failures - higher is better), MTTR (mean time to repair - lower is better) and repeat faults (the same fault coming back means the root cause was not fixed).
Theory

Inspection Routines

An inspection routine is a planned, written check of the robot's condition. It is done the same way every time so that nothing is missed and the results can be compared over time.

Mechanical condition

  • Fixings - are all screws, servo horns and brackets tight? A loose servo horn lets a joint slip, which shifts its zero.
  • Play and backlash - with torque on, gently try to move each link. Any free movement before the servo resists is play in the gears or a loose joint.
  • Cables - look for wear, kinks or crushed insulation where the servo bus cable flexes at each joint; check every connector is fully seated.
  • End effector - gripper fingers, linkages and the gripper servo.
  • Base - is the arm still firmly fixed to the bench? A base that moves ruins accuracy.

Theory

Spares, Tools and Safe Isolation

Spare parts

A broken robot that is waiting for a part is not producing anything. Factories hold a stock of critical spares - parts that wear out, fail most often, or would take longest to get. For an industrial robot this typically includes seals, bearings, motors, pneumatic and hydraulic lines and connectors, and end-effector parts such as gripper fingers and suction cups.

For this arm, sensible spares are: a spare ST3215 servo (it must be given the correct ID and commissioned before use), servo bus cables and connectors, fuses for the power supply, gripper parts including the small gripper servo, and a set of fixings.

Tools and test equipment

Hand tools

Hex (Allen) keys, screwdrivers, spanners and socket sets for fixings. Industrial robots also need a grease gun for gearboxes - the ST3215 servos are sealed, so this arm does not.

Test equipment

A multimeter to check supply voltage, continuity of cables and fuses, and to prove a circuit is dead. A safe isolation kit: lock-off device, padlock, warning tag and a voltage tester.

Mastering gauges and reference marks

Alignment marks, pins or gauges that show exactly where each joint's zero is - see the calibration section below.

Software tools

The app's Calibration tab reads every setting stored in a servo. The project also has a bench calibration jig used to set up new servos.

⚡
Safe isolation before any hands-on maintenance
Never work inside a robot's envelope, or on its wiring, with power applied. The safe isolation procedure is: 1. Identify the supply. 2. Isolate it (switch off and disconnect). 3. Lock off and tag it so no one can reconnect it. 4. Prove dead with a tested voltage tester. 5. Check the tester still works on a known live source. On this arm, isolating the 24 V supply also powers down the onboard computer. Safe isolation is how employers meet the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989; mains-powered equipment such as the arm's power supply is also inspected and tested at intervals (portable appliance testing, PAT).

Standard Operating Procedures

A standard operating procedure (SOP) is a written, step-by-step method for a task, so that it is done safely and the same way by everyone. A good maintenance SOP contains:

Activity 15A

Carry Out an Inspection

Work in pairs. One person carries out each check; the other records it in the worksheet. Visual checks first, with the arm stopped and torque off.

  • With the arm powered down, inspect every fixing, cable and connector you can see. Do not dismantle anything - record anything loose or damaged and report it.
  • Power up and connect. In Connection, record the server bus diagnostics. In Joint Control, record each joint's temperature and voltage at rest.
  • Take control and switch torque on. Gently try to move each link by hand and feel for play. Record any joint with noticeable free movement.
  • Look at the environment around the arm: dust, heat sources, blocked ventilation, cables near power wiring. Record what you find.
Theory

Calibration and Mastering

Every move a robot makes depends on it knowing where each joint's zero is. If a joint's zero is out by just 1°, the tool tip of a 300 mm arm misses by about 5 mm - and every stored position, program and XYZ move is wrong by the same amount.

A joint's zero can move after a servo is replaced, after a servo horn slips on its spline, or after a crash. Setting the zero back to the correct physical position is called mastering (or zero calibration). Industrial manufacturers each have their own method - for example alignment (witness) marks, a dial gauge in a reference notch, or an electronic mastering device - but the principle is always the same: put the joint at a known physical position, then tell the controller that this is zero.

Where the arm stores its calibration

🔒 The servo's own calibration (teacher)

Each ST3215 stores a Position Correction value in its EEPROM (address 0x1F). It stays in the servo when the power is off. Changing it needs the instructor's password, because a mistake affects every user of the arm.

🎓 The trainee trim (you)

A software-only offset kept by the onboard computer, on top of the servo's own calibration. It is limited to ±10° and Reset always removes it completely - so you can calibrate for real without any risk of losing the arm's true calibration.

Tool and base reference points

Mastering gets the joints right. Industrial cells also check the tool centre point (TCP) and base references - typically by bringing the tool tip to a fixed pointer in the cell and checking the reported XYZ position matches. A wrong tool length or a base that has moved shows up as a consistent XYZ error even when every joint's zero is correct.

Activity 15B

Explore the Servo Registers (Read-Only)

The Calibration tab reads every setting stored in a servo's memory. You can look at all of them - writing is locked behind the instructor's password.

  • Take control of the arm, open the Calibration tab, choose Joint 1 and press Read Registers.
  • The EEPROM table holds settings that stay in the servo when it is powered off. Rows that differ from the factory default are highlighted. Find Position correction (0x1F) and the Min / Max angle limit (0x09 / 0x0B) and record them in the worksheet.
  • Tick Show only values that differ from factory default. What has been changed from the factory settings, and why do you think it was changed?
  • The SRAM table is live data that is lost at power-off. Find Current location, Current voltage and Current temperature, and compare them with the Joint Control tab.
  • Repeat for the other joints. Do all six servos have the same Position correction? Why might they not?
🔑
Why some registers are never editable
Even with the password, the servo ID and baud rate cannot be changed from this tab. A wrong ID or baud rate would make the servo stop answering on the bus, and you would need the bench calibration jig to recover it.
Activity 15C

Calibrate a Joint's Zero

You will master one joint using its alignment marks and the trainee trim, check your result, then undo it.

⚠️
Before you start
Clear the area around the arm and keep a hand near the emergency stop. Jog with torque on and the smallest step size - with torque off, the shoulder and elbow can drop under their own weight. Your trim affects everyone using this arm until it is reset, so always finish with step 8.
  • As found. In Joint Control, command your joint to 0°. Look closely at its alignment marks. Do they line up? Record how far out they look.
  • Align. Jog the joint in the smallest steps until the marks line up exactly. Record the angle the app now shows - this is the joint's zero error.
  • Set. In Calibration, select the same joint and press Set current position as 0°. The joint does not move, but its reading becomes 0°. Note the trim shown in the trainee trims line.
  • Verify. Command the joint to +30°, then back to 0°. Do the marks still line up? Repeat from -30°. This tests whether the zero is right from both directions (any difference is backlash).
  • Check the effect. Watch the 3D view and its End Effector Position readout as you set and reset the trim. Why does a zero error matter for XYZ moves as well as joint moves?
  • Record as left. Fill in the calibration record in the worksheet - this is exactly what a maintenance engineer would sign off.
  • Compare. Your instructor may show you how the same correction would be made permanently, using the Position Correction register.
  • Undo. Press Reset this joint. Check that the trainee trims line says none active and that the joint's reading has gone back to the value you recorded in step 2.

So What?

Why Does This Matter in Industry?

On a car production line, one robot stopping can stop the whole line - downtime can cost thousands of pounds a minute. Maintenance teams are judged on how rarely that happens, and calibration teams on whether a robot that has had a motor replaced goes back into production placing parts exactly where it did before.

The habits you practised here are the professional ones: inspect against a written routine, record as found and as left, isolate before you touch, verify your work with an acceptance test, and make changes in a way that can be undone. That last point is why the trainee trim exists - and why industrial controllers keep backups of their calibration data before anyone remasters a robot.
