📖 Theory

5.1 — Forces and Moments in a Static System

When the robot arm is stationary (in static equilibrium), the forces and moments acting on it are balanced. Understanding these forces tells us how much torque each joint servo must produce just to hold the arm in position — before any load is picked up or any motion occurs.

Key concepts:

  • Force (F) — a push or pull, measured in Newtons (N). Gravity acts downward on every mass: F = m × g (where g ≈ 9.81 m/s²).
  • Moment (M) — a turning force about a pivot, measured in Newton-metres (Nm). M = F × d, where d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.
  • Torque — another name for a moment; specifically the rotational force a servo must produce at its shaft to counteract external moments.
M = F × d Moment (Nm) = Force (N) × Perpendicular distance (m)

Why the elbow and shoulder joints work hardest

Consider the arm held horizontally. The weight of Link 3, Link 4, Link 5 and Link 6 acts at their centres of mass, which are far from the shoulder joint. The moment arm (distance from shoulder to the centre of mass) is large, so even a small mass creates a significant moment at the shoulder. The elbow joint supports only the links above it, so its moment is smaller — but still significant.

💡
Example:
Link 3 has mass 0.2 kg and its centre of mass is 0.15 m from the elbow joint. The moment at the elbow due to Link 3's weight = 0.2 × 9.81 × 0.15 = 0.29 Nm. The shoulder must also support this moment plus the moments from all links above.
📊
Reading Load from the App
The Joint Control tab shows a load (%) field on each joint's status card. This is the servo's measured current as a percentage of its stall torque (the maximum torque it can produce before stalling). Because torque = motor constant × current, a high load % means the servo is working hard to maintain its position — it is the earliest warning of mechanical overload. Watch the load % change as you move the arm between poses in Activity 5A.

5.2 — Free Body Diagrams

A free body diagram (FBD) is a sketch that isolates a single component and shows all the forces and moments acting on it. It is the standard engineering tool for setting up equilibrium equations.

For a robot arm joint, the FBD shows:

  • The weight forces of all links above the joint (acting at their centres of mass)
  • Any payload weight at the end effector
  • The joint reaction force and the joint torque (produced by the servo)

For static equilibrium: sum of all forces = 0 and sum of all moments = 0.

⚠️
Pose-dependent loading
The moment at each joint depends heavily on the arm's pose. A joint carries maximum load when the arm is fully extended horizontally; it carries near-zero bending moment when the arm is straight up (vertical). This is why picking up a heavy object at full arm extension is much harder on the servos than the same pick close to the base.
🔧 Software Activity

Activity 5A — Measure how load changes with arm pose

App tab: Joint Control

  1. In the Joint Control tab, read the load (%) value from each of the 6 joint status cards. This is the live current load as a percentage of stall torque. Record all 6 values in the table below for each pose: (a) arm fully horizontal, (b) arm at 45° elevation, (c) arm pointing straight up.

  2. Position the arm so it is roughly horizontal — Joint 2 (shoulder) extended out to the side, elbow and wrist straight. Record the load readings for joints 1, 2, and 3.

  3. Now move the arm to a vertical pose — all joints pointing upward. Record the load readings again. Compare them to the horizontal pose.

  4. Move Joint 2 to 45° from horizontal. Record the load. You should see it is between the horizontal and vertical values. Note which joint has the highest load in each pose.

  5. If you have a small object (under 100 g), hold it at the end effector while the arm is horizontal and record the change in load. Carefully remove the object. Note: keep the workspace clear and handle objects carefully.

  6. Enable the Joint Traces overlay in the 3D Visualisation tab, then pop out the 3D view into a separate window (Pop Out button). Arrange the 3D window and the Joint Control tab side by side. Move the arm between the three poses and observe how the load % values in Joint Control change as the arm configuration changes on screen — this makes the moment-arm relationship directly visible.

📊
The load reading from the servo is a proxy for motor current, which is proportional to the torque being produced. It is not a direct Nm reading, but it is useful for comparing relative loading across poses.
🔬
Extension — End-Tool Force Sensing
The ESP32 end-tool node has ADC current-sensing inputs. If a gripper is fitted, the grip current is a direct measure of grip force. Calculate the moment at Joint 6 from the grip current reading (visible via the end-tool panel in Joint Control) and compare it to the moment calculated from the physical payload mass. This links electrical measurement directly to a statics calculation.

💭 So What?

Why does this matter?

Every servo in the arm has a rated maximum torque. If a pose or payload exceeds that limit, the servo will stall, overheat, and ultimately fail. By calculating the moments in advance, an engineer can determine whether a design is safe before anything is built — or set software limits on arm extension and payload to prevent overloading the hardware.

The load readings you observed in the app are the real-time expression of these calculations. Experienced operators learn to read load trends: a joint that is consistently near its limit in a particular pose is a servo that will fail prematurely. Redesigning the path to avoid that pose extends hardware life dramatically.
