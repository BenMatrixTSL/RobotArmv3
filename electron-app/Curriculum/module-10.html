📖 Theory

10.1 - The Camera as a Sensor

In Module 3 you met tactile and non-tactile sensors. Most of them answer one simple question: is something there? or how far away is it? A camera is a non-tactile sensor that can answer far richer questions at the same time - what is there, where it is, which way it is facing, and whether it looks right.

Using cameras and software to make decisions in a machine is called machine vision. In industrial robotics it is used for three main jobs:

JobQuestion it answersIndustrial examples
Inspection Is this part correct? Checking labels are straight, finding scratches or missing components, measuring that a hole is the right size, rejecting faulty bottles on a filling line.
Identification What is this part? Reading barcodes, QR codes and printed text (OCR), sorting parts by colour or shape, recognising which product variant has arrived.
Guidance & navigation Where is it, and how do I get there? Robot picking of parts placed at random on a conveyor, bin picking, mobile robots (AMRs) finding their way around a factory, lining up a part before welding.

The robot arm's camera does two of these jobs: identification (it recognises a block's colour) and guidance (it works out where the block is in millimetres so the arm can pick it). Inspection is something you will think about in the worksheet.

🎯
Why vision matters for robots
Without a sensor, a robot can only pick a part from a position it has been taught. If the part is 10 mm out of place, the robot misses it. Vision lets a robot handle variable part positions - the robot measures where each part actually is, then adapts its move. This is the step from simple automation to adaptive automation you met in Module 6.
📖 Theory

10.2 - How the Arm's Vision System Works

The arm uses an ordinary USB webcam plugged into the onboard computer, looking down at a mat on the work surface. A separate program on the onboard computer - the vision service - reads the camera, finds markers and blocks in each image, and makes the results available to the app.

An image is a grid of measurements

A digital image is a grid of tiny squares called pixels. A 1280 × 720 image has 921,600 of them. Each pixel stores three numbers from 0 to 255 - how much Red, Green and Blue light reached that point of the sensor. So a single camera image is nearly 3 million measurements, and vision software's job is to turn all of those numbers into a few useful answers, such as "one red block at X = 120 mm, Y = 85 mm".

Resolution - more pixels means finer detail, but more processing Detection rate - about 5 analyses per second Field of view - how much of the table the camera can see
⏱️
Vision is slower than the servos
The servo status updates every 20 ms (50 times a second), but the vision system analyses about 5 images a second - one every 200 ms. Vision takes much more processing than reading a number from a servo. This is why robots usually stop, look, then move rather than steering continuously by camera.

Activity 10A - Explore the Camera Tab

App tab: Camera

Get to know what the vision system can see and what it reports. You need the arm connected (Module 3) and the camera mat with its four corner markers in view.

  1. Open the Camera tab. The camera starts automatically. Read the status line above the picture - it tells you how many ArUco markers and coloured blocks are visible.

  2. Look at the overlays drawn on the image: a green outline and "id" label on each marker, a cyan outline around the mat, and two arrows - red for X and green for Y. Sketch where the origin (0, 0) is and which way X and Y point.

  3. Place three or four blocks of different colours on the mat. Each one should get a yellow box and an index number. Record the Detected blocks table (index, colour, X, Y, rotation) in the worksheet.

  4. Slide one block towards the top of the picture, past another block. Watch the index numbers - blocks are numbered from the top of the picture downwards, so the numbers can swap when blocks move.

  5. Cover one corner marker with a sheet of paper. What changes on screen? Now cover a second marker. Record what happens to the block positions.

📖 Theory

10.3 - Finding Coloured Blocks

The vision service finds blocks in four steps:

  1. Convert to HSV. Each pixel's RGB values are converted into Hue (which colour - red, yellow, green…), Saturation (how strong the colour is) and Value (how bright it is).
  2. Threshold. For each colour it looks for pixels whose hue, saturation and value fall inside a set range. The result is a black-and-white "mask" showing only the matching pixels. The arm looks for red, green, blue, yellow and purple.
  3. Filter by shape. Patches that are too small (noise) or too big (a shirt sleeve), not roughly square, or full of holes are thrown away, and so is anything outside the mat. What is left should be the top faces of blocks.
  4. Check it is stable. A block must be seen in 5 frames in a row before it is reported, and missing for 5 frames before it is removed. This stops a block flickering in and out of the list.

Why HSV and not RGB?

When light gets brighter or dimmer, all three RGB numbers change together, so a red block in shadow can have very different RGB values from the same block in sunlight. In HSV, a change in brightness mostly changes only the Value - the Hue stays roughly the same. That makes "find the red things" far more reliable.

💡
Lighting is the biggest cause of vision problems
Shadows, glare from windows, reflections from shiny surfaces and coloured backgrounds can all break colour detection. This is why industrial vision systems use controlled lighting - ring lights around the lens, backlights behind the part, or a cover that blocks out daylight - instead of relying on room lights.
🔧 Software Activity

Activity 10B - Test the Limits of Colour Detection

App tab: Camera

Find out when colour detection works and when it fails. Record each test in the worksheet.

  1. With normal room lighting, place one block of each colour on the mat and check they are all detected with the correct colour.

  2. Shadow: hold a book above the mat so a shadow falls across some blocks. Are they still detected? Is the colour still correct?

  3. Glare: shine a phone torch at the mat from the side. What happens to blocks in the bright spot?

  4. Distractors: put a coloured object that is not a block on the mat (a pen, a coloured sticky note, a crumpled wrapper). Is it wrongly detected as a block? Why or why not?

  5. Response time: place a new block on the mat and count how long it takes to appear in the table. Use the 5-frame rule and the detection rate to explain the delay.

📖 Theory

10.4 - From Pixels to Millimetres: ArUco Markers

Finding a block at "pixel 640, 360" is no use to a robot - it needs to know where the block is in millimetres. To convert, the vision system needs some points in the picture whose real positions it already knows. That is the job of the ArUco markers.

What is an ArUco marker?

An ArUco marker is a black square with a pattern of black and white cells inside (the arm uses a 4 × 4 pattern). Each pattern encodes an ID number. Markers like this are called fiducial markers - reference points that are deliberately easy for software to find: very high contrast, sharp straight edges, and a code that cannot be confused with anything else in the scene.

Building the mat's coordinate frame

  • Four markers sit at the corners of the mat. The software knows the real distance between their centres (by default 240 mm across and 230 mm down).
  • It works out which marker is at which corner from where they appear in the picture, so only the four corner markers should be in view - an extra marker confuses it.
  • The top-left marker is the origin (0, 0). X increases to the right and Y increases down the picture - the red and green arrows you saw in Activity 10A.
  • With all four markers visible it calculates a perspective transform. This corrects for the camera looking at the mat at an angle, where the far edge of the mat appears shorter than the near edge.
  • With only three markers it falls back to an affine transform, which can stretch and rotate but cannot correct perspective - positions become less accurate, and the screen warns you. With fewer than three markers it cannot convert to millimetres, so no blocks are reported at all.
📐
Link to Module 7
This is the same idea as the coordinate frames in Module 7. The camera has its own frame (pixels, origin top-left of the picture), the mat has a frame (mm, origin at a marker), and the robot has a base frame (mm, origin at its base). Vision-guided picking only works if you can convert positions correctly from one frame to the next.
🔍
Research further: camera calibration
Real camera lenses bend straight lines slightly, especially near the edges of the picture (lens distortion). Industrial systems correct this with camera calibration: photographing a printed chessboard from many angles to measure the lens. This arm's system does not do lens calibration, so blocks near the edges of the picture may be less accurate. Search for "camera calibration chessboard OpenCV" to see how it is done.
🔧 Practical Activity

Activity 10C - How Accurate Is the Camera?

App tab: Camera

Compare the camera's measurement with a ruler - the same way an engineer checks any sensor against a trusted reference.

  1. Place a single block on the mat. With a ruler, measure from the centre of the top-left marker to the centre of the block: X across, Y down. This is your reference measurement.

  2. Read the block's X and Y from the Detected blocks table. Record both in the worksheet.

  3. Work out the error in each direction, then the total position error: error = √(ΔX² + ΔY²).

  4. Repeat with the block near the centre of the mat and near a far corner. Is the camera more accurate in some places than others?

  5. Cover one marker so only three are visible, and measure the far-corner block again. How much worse is the three-marker result?

📖 Theory

10.5 - From the Camera to the Robot

Knowing where a block is on the mat is still not quite enough - the robot moves in its own base frame. Working out the exact relationship between the camera's frame and the robot's frame is called hand-eye calibration (or camera-to-robot calibration). In industry, this is done by moving the robot to several known points the camera can see, then calculating the transform between them.

This arm takes a simpler approach: it assumes the mat frame and the robot's frame are the same - the same origin and the same X and Y directions. When a program picks a block, the app takes the block's X and Y from the camera, adds a pick height Z that you choose, and uses inverse kinematics (working backwards from a tool position to the joint angles, the reverse of the forward kinematics in Module 7) to work out the joint angles.

⚠️
Always check before you trust it
Nothing in the app checks that the mat is placed exactly where the robot thinks it is. If the mat is shifted or turned slightly, every pick will be off by the same amount - a systematic error. Always check one pick slowly, from above, before running a vision-guided program at speed.

What the camera does not tell the robot

  • Height: the camera sees from above, so it cannot tell how tall a block is. You supply the pick height (Z) in your program; it defaults to table level.
  • Orientation for the gripper: the camera measures each block's rotation (shown in the table), but the programming commands do not use it, so the gripper always approaches at the same angle.

There are two ways to move to a block:

  • Read X and Y into variables, then do a Cartesian move to them - e.g. in G-code G1 X#2 Y#3 Z60.
  • Save the block into a Stored Position slot, then move to that slot like any taught position - e.g. G1 P10 or MoveToPos 10;.

Example - pick the first block (G-code)

M10 ; open the gripper M780 V1 ; how many blocks can the camera see? → #1 M782 P0 V2 ; X of block 0 → #2 M783 P0 V3 ; Y of block 0 → #3 G1 X#2 Y#3 Z60 F1200 ; move above the block G1 X#2 Y#3 Z15 F600 ; move down to grip height M11 ; close the gripper G1 X#2 Y#3 Z60 F600 ; lift the block clear

The heights 60 mm and 15 mm are examples only. Your instructor will give you safe approach and grip heights for your arm, blocks and gripper.

🎨
Sorting by colour
Only Blockly can read a block's colour. To build a colour-sorting program, use Blockly: loop through each block index, check block colour at index, and choose a different drop-off position for each colour.
🔧 Practical Activity

Activity 10D - Your First Vision-Guided Pick

App tabs: Camera · G-Code Control · Blockly

Check that the camera and robot agree, then pick a block that the robot has never been taught.

🛑
Safety first
You have control of the arm. Use a low speed, keep everyone outside the arm's working envelope, and keep a hand near the emergency stop. Always move to a point above the block first - never straight down to the table on a first attempt.

  1. Check the frames agree. Place one block on the mat. Run M781 P0 L10 Z60 to save a point 60 mm above it into slot 10, then move to slot 10 slowly (G1 P10 F20). Look down from above: is the gripper directly over the block? Measure and record any offset in X and Y.

  2. Move the block to a different place on the mat and repeat. Is the offset the same each time (a systematic error - the mat is out of place) or different each time (a random error - the measurement is noisy)?

  3. Tell your instructor if the offset is more than a few millimetres. The mat may need repositioning before you continue.

  4. Pick the block. Enter the example G-code from section 10.6 using your instructor's safe heights. Run it with the speed turned down and watch the first approach carefully.

  5. Extension: in Blockly, build a program that picks every block the camera can see and places each one at a drop-off position. Then try making it sort by colour.

💭 So What?

Why does this matter?

Vision is one of the fastest-growing areas of industrial automation. A robot without vision needs every part presented in exactly the same place, which means expensive jigs, fixtures and feeders. A robot with vision can cope with parts that arrive in random positions - and the same camera can check the quality of every part at the same time.

The system you used today is simple, but every idea in it scales up to industry: controlled lighting, reference markers to calibrate the camera, coordinate transforms from pixels to the robot's frame, and checking accuracy against a trusted reference. Industrial systems add lens calibration, 3D cameras that measure height, and hand-eye calibration routines - but the reasoning is exactly what you have just practised.

Notice, too, that vision did not replace the other sensors. The servos' encoders still close the loop on every joint; the camera only decides where to go. Real robot cells combine several types of sensor, each doing the job it is best at.
