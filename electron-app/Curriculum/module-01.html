📖 Theory

1.1 — What is a Robot Arm?

A robot arm (also called a robotic manipulator) is a mechanical device that can move and position objects in three-dimensional space. Unlike a human arm, it follows instructions exactly — it will not stop if you get in the way.

Key parts of any robot arm:

  • Base — the fixed mounting point (like your shoulder socket)
  • Links — the rigid sections that connect one joint to the next (like your upper arm and forearm)
  • Joints — the connection points that allow rotation (like your elbow and wrist)
  • End Effector — the tool at the tip of the arm that does the actual work (like your hand)
🔩
Degrees of Freedom (DoF)
Each joint gives the arm one extra degree of freedom — one more independent way to move. Our robot arm has 6 joints, meaning it has 6 degrees of freedom. More DoF = more flexible, but also more complex to control.

Where are robot arms used?

  • Pick-and-place — moving components along a production line
  • Welding & painting — consistent, repeatable finishing
  • Assembly — inserting screws, placing circuit boards
  • Inspection — holding a camera over a product
  • Surgery assistance — precise movements inside the body
🔧 Software Activity

Activity 1A — Explore the arm structure in 3D

App tab: 3D Visualisation

Open the Robot Arm Control Application and navigate to the 3D Visualisation tab. You should see a rendered model of the arm.

  1. Launch the Robot Arm application on your PC (or run it from the robot arm touchscreen).

  2. Click the 3D Visualisation tab in the top navigation bar.

  3. Use your mouse to rotate the view: left-click + drag to orbit, scroll wheel to zoom, right-click + drag to pan.

  4. Count the number of visible joints (pivot points shown as cylinders or spheres) in the model. Write this number in the worksheet below.

  5. Count the number of links (the rigid segments between joints). Write this too.

  6. Look at the end effector at the tip of the arm. Note its shape and what it might be used for.

  7. In the toolbar above the 3D view, enable the Movement Envelope overlay. You should see a sphere or volume appear around the arm showing the full space it can reach. This is the working envelope — never enter this volume with torque enabled.

  8. Enable the End-Effector Trace overlay, then use the simulation mode sliders (bottom of the 3D view panel) to sweep a joint angle. Watch the trace plot the path the tool tip would follow. This overlay will be used in later modules to visualise planned paths.

📖 Theory

1.2 — Why Safety is the First Priority

Robot arms are designed to move with force and speed. They do not have built-in awareness of people nearby. A servo motor can generate significant torque — enough to cause a crush injury. Electrical hazards also exist around the power supply and wiring.

⚠️
The robot will not stop for you.
Unlike a person, a robot arm executes its programmed motion regardless of what is in the way. Never assume it will detect your presence and stop.

The three main hazard categories:

  • Pinch and crush points — gaps between moving links and joints that can trap fingers or hands
  • Unexpected or rapid motion — the arm moving suddenly when you are too close, or faster than expected after a speed change
  • Electrical hazards — exposed connections, damaged cables, or operating near liquids

The working envelope

The working envelope is the full volume of space the arm can reach. Never enter the working envelope while the arm has torque enabled. Even if the arm appears to be stationary, a signal could cause it to move at any time.

Enabling and disabling torque

Each joint is driven by a servo motor. Torque is the rotational force that moves the joint. When torque is disabled, the joints can be moved freely by hand and the arm is safe to approach. When torque is enabled, the arm is under active control and must be treated as live machinery.

💡
LED Status Strip
The arm has a coloured LED strip that shows its current state at a glance. Always check the strip colour before approaching the arm. grey = torque disabled (safe to approach), solid green = connected and ready, red = fault or error, flashing orange = thermal overtemperature fault, yellow comet animation = arm is actively moving.
🔐
Control Session — Software Lockout
Only one computer can send movement commands to the arm at a time. Before issuing any movement, press Take Control in the Connection tab. This is the software equivalent of a physical lockout/tagout — other users can observe the arm's status but cannot move it until you release control. Always press Release Control when you have finished, and release control before approaching the arm.
⚠️
Emergency Stop procedure
If the arm moves unexpectedly, or someone is at risk: stop the program immediately using the application's stop control, then power down the arm. Investigate before restarting. Never restart without understanding why the unexpected motion occurred.
🔧 Software Activity

Activity 1B — Locate the safety controls in the app

App tab: Joint Control

Before ever moving the arm, you must be able to find and use the torque controls. Do this activity with the arm powered but stationary and everyone clear of the working envelope.

  1. Navigate to the Joint Control tab.

  2. Find the Torque Enable / Disable toggle or button. Do not click it yet — just locate it and note where it is in the worksheet.

  3. With the arm powered and everyone clear, click Disable Torque. Gently move Joint 1 (the base rotation joint) by hand a few degrees. Notice how it moves freely.

  4. Click Enable Torque and immediately step back outside the working envelope. The arm may return to its programmed position.

  5. Practice this disable → check clear → enable sequence three times until it feels natural.

  6. Navigate to the Pendant Control tab. Locate the large red E-Stop button. On the robot arm touchscreen (pendant mode), this is the primary emergency stop. Note that E-stop buttons also appear in the Joint Control tab — familiarise yourself with both locations.

💡
This disable → approach → enable workflow is the correct way to manually reposition or inspect the arm. Always confirm that everyone is clear before re-enabling torque.

1.3 — Risk Assessment

A risk assessment is a structured process for identifying what could go wrong and deciding how to reduce those risks to an acceptable level before starting work. It is a legal requirement in most industrial settings and good practice everywhere.

The five steps:

  1. Identify the hazards — what could cause harm?
  2. Decide who might be harmed — operators, bystanders, visitors?
  3. Evaluate the risk — how likely is harm, and how severe?
  4. Record and implement control measures — what will you do to reduce risk?
  5. Review and update — does anything change the risk level over time?

Risk rating matrix

Risk is assessed as a combination of Likelihood (how often it could happen) and Severity (how bad the harm would be):

💡 So What?

Why does this matter?

Every serious robotics accident in industry happened because someone made an assumption — that the arm was off, that it would stop in time, that no one was in the way. The procedures in this module exist because of real incidents.

The software application you are using gives you direct control over a real machine. Every time you open it, the same rules apply: check the workspace is clear, know where the stop control is, and never assume the arm is safe just because it looks still.

As you progress through this curriculum, the tasks become more complex. The safety fundamentals from Module 1 remain constant. Professionals in robotics cells rehearse emergency procedures regularly — not because accidents are likely, but because when they happen they happen fast.
