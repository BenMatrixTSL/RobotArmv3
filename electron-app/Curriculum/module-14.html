Theory

Engineering Project Phases

Most engineering projects follow a common lifecycle, regardless of scale. Understanding which phase you are in helps you focus your effort and communicate progress clearly:

🔍 1. Brief & Scope

What needs to be built? Who is it for? What are the constraints (time, budget, skills)?

📄 2. Research & Specification

Investigate options, define measurable requirements (the spec), identify risks.

✎️ 3. Design

Sketch, model, simulate. Select the best approach. Document decisions.

🔧 4. Prototype & Build

Make the first version. Accept that it will not be perfect — iteration is normal.

✅ 5. Test & Evaluate

Test against the specification. Record results. Identify what to improve.

📄 6. Document & Present

Write up what you did and why. Present findings to a technical or non-technical audience.

Gantt Charts & Time Planning

A Gantt chart is a bar chart that maps tasks against time. It is the most widely used project scheduling tool in engineering. Each row is a task; the bar shows when it runs. Overlapping bars show parallel work.

Notice that design and build overlap in weeks 3–4 — this is normal in iterative engineering. The chart makes dependencies visible: you cannot build before you have designed.

Theory

Writing a Specification

A specification defines what a successful outcome looks like, in measurable terms. Vague requirements lead to disagreements about whether the project succeeded.

Vague requirementMeasurable specification
"The arm should be accurate"Repeat position accuracy ≤ ±2° on all joints after 10 cycles
"The arm should be safe"All joint limits configured; risk assessment completed and signed; SOP posted at workstation
"The program should work"G-code program completes 5 consecutive cycles without fault; cycle time ≤ 15 s
"Good documentation"Circuit diagram, wiring list, and annotated code submitted; peer-reviewed
Tip: Use Essential and Desirable columns in your spec. Essential = must have for the project to pass. Desirable = would improve it if time allows.
✅
Measurable Specification Points — Examples for This Arm
  • "All 6 joints return to the Home position within ±1° after 10 consecutive cycles" — measurable via the angle readings in Joint Control
  • "End-to-end cycle time (Home → Pick → Place → Home) is under 12 seconds at 75% speed" — measurable with a stopwatch or the execution log
  • "App connects to Pi and achieves a control session within 5 seconds on the classroom network" — measurable from the Connection tab
  • "Joint temperatures remain below 50°C after 10 consecutive cycles" — measurable from the temperature field in Joint Control
  • "Camera tab identifies a red block on a white surface within 2 seconds of placement" — measurable with a timer and the Camera tab
Compare these to the generic examples above: each references a specific data source (a tab, a field, a tool) so the measurement method is unambiguous.
Worksheet

Activity — Plan Your Robot Arm Project

You will create a project plan for your own robot arm assignment. This plan will guide your work in Modules 15 and 16.

  • Write a project brief in 3–5 sentences: what you are going to make the arm do, who it is for, and what success looks like.
  • List at least six measurable specification points (mix of essential and desirable) covering: performance, safety, software, and documentation.
  • Identify at least three risks to your project (technical or practical) and write a mitigation for each.
  • Draw a Gantt chart on paper or in a spreadsheet, mapping your six project phases across the weeks available. Identify any parallel tasks.
  • Share your plan with a classmate for peer review. Ask them: Is the brief clear? Are the spec points measurable? Is the timeline realistic?
  • Update your plan based on their feedback and keep it as a living document — revise it as the project progresses.
🔧
Extension Scope — End-Tool Subsystem
If you want a more advanced project, include the end-tool subsystem (gripper or vacuum pump) in your specification. Additional specification points could include:
  • "Gripper closes to a consistent angle within ±5° across 10 operations" — verify via the End Tool Servo panel in Joint Control
  • "Vacuum pump maintains suction for at least 10 seconds without pressure loss" — verify by timing grip duration during the pick operation
The end-tool controls (GripperOpen, GripperClose, PumpOn, PumpOff) are available in all three programming modes (G-Code: M10/M11; RAPID: named commands; Blockly: dedicated blocks).

Risk Register — Software-Specific Risks to Include
  • Risk: Control session contention — two students try to command the arm simultaneously. Likelihood: Medium. Mitigation: single-client enforcement by the server; 5-minute idle timeout; communicate who has control before switching operators.
  • Risk: Network drop mid-move — the app loses connection while a G-code sequence is running. Likelihood: Low. Mitigation: the server continues executing buffered commands; reconnect immediately and issue Stop All from the G-Code Control tab.
  • Risk: Serial bus loss during pick — the server loses contact with the servo bus while gripping. Likelihood: Very Low. Mitigation: auto-recovery occurs within 30 s; always Home before starting a new sequence to confirm arm position.
So What?

Why Planning Matters

Most engineering project failures are not caused by bad technical skills — they are caused by poor planning. Running out of time, scope creep (the project keeps growing), and unclear requirements are the top three causes of cost overruns and late delivery in the engineering industry.

The tools in this module — Gantt charts, specifications, risk registers — are used by professional engineers on projects worth millions of pounds. You are learning the same language.

Think about: Look back at your Gantt chart. What is your critical path — the sequence of tasks where any delay will push back the final deadline? How could you protect it?
